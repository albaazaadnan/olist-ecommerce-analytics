Project success criteria

Technical

  • Understand every source table and its grain.
  • Perform reproducible data profiling in Python.
  • Load validated data into PostgreSQL.
  • Use joins, CTEs, aggregations and window functions.
  • Build a clean analytical model in Power BI.
  • Create useful DAX measures rather than excessive calculated columns.

Business

  • Define a clear management problem.
  • Answer questions, not just make charts.
  • Identify meaningful patterns and exceptions.
  • Quantify findings.
  • Translate findings into recommendations.
  • Document assumptions and limitations.
Portfolio rule: A recruiter should be able to understand what the project does, why it matters, how you built it, and what you discovered within a few minutes of opening your GitHub repository.

04 • Data discovery

Profile every table before cleaning

Do not immediately merge tables. First inspect each source independently. You are learning the structure of the business.

For every table, answer these questions

Structure
  • How many rows?
  • How many columns?
  • What are the data types?
  • What is the row grain?
  • What appears to be the primary key?
Quality
  • How many nulls?
  • How many duplicates?
  • Are values outside expected ranges?
  • Are categories inconsistent?
  • Are dates valid?

Starter profiling code

import pandas as pd

df = pd.read_csv("data/raw/olist_orders_dataset.csv")

print(df.shape)
print(df.dtypes)
print(df.head())
print(df.isna().sum().sort_values(ascending=False))
print("Duplicate rows:", df.duplicated().sum())
print(df.nunique().sort_values())
print(df.describe(include="all").T)

Relationship discovery

Use unique counts and joins to test relationships rather than assuming them.

# Example: how many order IDs in order_items are missing from orders?
missing_orders = (
    set(order_items["order_id"].dropna())
    - set(orders["order_id"].dropna())
)
print(len(missing_orders))
Never “fix” an anomaly before understanding it. A duplicate row, null value or unusual quantity may be a legitimate business event. Investigate first; document the decision second.

05 • EDA

Exploratory Data Analysis

EDA is not a gallery of charts. Its purpose is to understand distributions, relationships, anomalies and business behavior before you build the analytical model.

EDA sequence

1
Univariate checks
Counts, distributions, missingness, unique values, date ranges and outliers.
2
Relationship checks
Orders ↔ items, orders ↔ customers, items ↔ products, items ↔ sellers.
3
Business EDA
Revenue trends, order volume, average order value, product/category performance, delivery and reviews.
4
Hypothesis generation
Write questions you want SQL/Power BI to answer rather than cherry-picking interesting charts.

Core metrics to establish

COUNT
SUM
SUM
AVG

Revenue definition

Choose a definition and use it consistently. A practical project metric is item value based on quantity × price. Be explicit about whether freight, payment totals, cancellations or other components are included.

item_value = quantity * price

Useful EDA questions

  • How many orders are delivered, canceled or unavailable?
  • How long is the purchase-to-delivery period?
  • Which categories dominate sales?
  • Does high sales volume correspond to high revenue?
  • Which states generate the most orders?
  • Do lower review scores coincide with longer delivery times?
  • How concentrated is revenue among customers/products/sellers?
EDA deliverable: finish this phase with a short “What I learned” section containing 5–10 observations and a list of data-quality issues that must be addressed.

06 • Data quality

Clean deliberately and document every decision

Do not aim for “zero nulls.” Aim for data that is fit for the business questions you are answering.

Data-quality categories

IssueDetectionPossible actionDocument?
Missing valuesisna()Keep, impute, or exclude depending on useYes
Duplicatesduplicated()Remove only confirmed duplicatesYes
Invalid datesto_datetime(errors="coerce")Investigate or isolateYes
Invalid numeric valuesRange rulesInvestigate source/business logicYes
Inconsistent labelsvalue_counts()Standardize categoriesYes
Broken relationshipsanti-joinsInvestigate orphan recordsYes

Recommended cleaning philosophy

  1. Preserve raw data. Never overwrite source files.
  2. Separate cleaning from analysis. Keep transformation code reproducible.
  3. Prefer business rules over arbitrary deletion.
  4. Track row counts before and after transformations.
  5. Validate totals. Cleaning should not silently change business metrics.

Optional portfolio enhancement: controlled messy-data exercise

Because Olist is already a prepared public dataset, you can create a controlled “raw simulation” for a small portion of the project: introduce documented examples of formatting inconsistencies or duplicate records, then show how your pipeline detects and fixes them. Do not misrepresent these injected issues as original Olist problems.

Cleaning checklist

  • Dates parsed consistently.
  • Numeric columns have correct types.
  • Text values normalized where appropriate.
  • Confirmed duplicates handled.
  • Null-handling decisions documented.
  • Foreign-key integrity checked.
  • Unexpected values investigated.
  • Before/after row counts recorded.
  • Business totals reconciled.
Do not fake complexity. Your project is already complex because the dataset is relational. Use injected issues only as a transparent learning exercise, not as fabricated evidence.

07 • PostgreSQL

Build the relational database layer

Olist already arrives as multiple related tables. Your job is to load them correctly, preserve their grain, define keys, and make the database reliable for analysis.

Recommended database layers

Raw / staging

Tables close to the source data. Minimal transformation.

Core

Validated relational tables with correct types and constraints.

Analytics

Views or derived tables designed for business analysis.

Conceptual relationship map

customers
   │
   └── orders
         │
         ├── order_items ─── products
         │        │
         │        └────────── sellers
         │
         ├── order_payments
         │
         └── order_reviews

products ─── category translation
customers / sellers ─── geography information

Example schema pattern

CREATE TABLE orders (
    order_id TEXT PRIMARY KEY,
    customer_id TEXT,
    order_status TEXT,
    order_purchase_timestamp TIMESTAMP,
    order_approved_at TIMESTAMP,
    order_delivered_carrier_date TIMESTAMP,
    order_delivered_customer_date TIMESTAMP,
    order_estimated_delivery_date TIMESTAMP
);

Adapt exact data types and constraints after inspecting the source. Do not blindly copy a schema without understanding the fields.

Loading sequence

  1. Create database and schemas.
  2. Create staging tables.
  3. Load CSV data.
  4. Validate row counts against source files.
  5. Transform types / standardize data.
  6. Populate core tables.
  7. Add primary/foreign keys after confirming integrity.
  8. Create analytical views.
Key principle: PostgreSQL is where you demonstrate relational-data competence. Power BI should not be the first place where the business data becomes understandable.

08 • SQL

Turn the database into analysis

Write SQL around business questions. Your SQL folder should tell the story of the analysis.

SQL progression

Foundation
  • SELECT / WHERE
  • GROUP BY / HAVING
  • ORDER BY
  • CASE
  • JOINs
Portfolio-level
  • CTEs
  • Window functions
  • Date calculations
  • Percent contribution
  • Period-over-period growth

Example: monthly revenue

SELECT
    DATE_TRUNC('month', o.order_purchase_timestamp) AS month,
    SUM(oi.price) AS revenue
FROM orders o
JOIN order_items oi
  ON o.order_id = oi.order_id
WHERE o.order_status = 'delivered'
GROUP BY 1
ORDER BY 1;

Example: product ranking

WITH product_sales AS (
    SELECT
        oi.product_id,
        SUM(oi.price) AS revenue
    FROM order_items oi
    GROUP BY oi.product_id
)
SELECT
    product_id,
    revenue,
    RANK() OVER (ORDER BY revenue DESC) AS revenue_rank
FROM product_sales
ORDER BY revenue_rank;

Business-analysis query checklist

  • Monthly revenue and order trend.
  • Revenue by product category.
  • Top and bottom products by revenue.
  • Top sellers.
  • Revenue by customer location.
  • Average order value.
  • Orders per customer.
  • Repeat-customer rate.
  • Delivery duration.
  • Review score by delivery performance.
  • Year-over-year or month-over-month growth where the date range supports it.
Interview target: Be able to explain at least one complex query line-by-line. It is better to understand 15 strong queries than to have 100 queries you cannot explain.

09 • Analytical model

Design the Power BI semantic model

This is where your previous Power BI experience becomes useful. But now you are consuming a database that you deliberately designed and validated.

Do not confuse the two models

PostgreSQL model

Optimized for reliable relational storage, integrity and SQL analysis.

PK/FKConstraintsViews

Power BI model

Optimized for interactive analytical queries, measures and report performance.

Star schemaDAXRelationships

Target analytical shape

                 DimDate
                    │
                    │
DimCustomer ─── FactOrderItems ─── DimProduct
                    │
                    │
                DimSeller
                    │
                    │
              DimGeography

Supporting facts:
• Order lifecycle
• Payments
• Reviews

Do not force every Olist source table into one enormous Power BI model. Decide which facts and dimensions are necessary for your dashboard and keep the model understandable.

Model checklist

  • Identify fact-table grain explicitly.
  • Create a proper Date table.
  • Use one-to-many relationships where appropriate.
  • Avoid unnecessary many-to-many relationships.
  • Avoid ambiguous filter paths.
  • Hide technical key columns from report users where appropriate.
  • Create measures for core KPIs.
  • Format currencies, percentages and dates consistently.
  • Test totals against PostgreSQL queries.

Core DAX measures

Total Revenue = SUM(FactOrderItems[price])

Orders = DISTINCTCOUNT(FactOrderItems[order_id])

Items Sold = SUM(FactOrderItems[order_item_count])

Average Order Value =
DIVIDE([Total Revenue], [Orders])

Visual design rules

Hierarchy

Most important KPI and trend should be visually dominant.

Consistency

Use the same units, date logic, labels and number formatting everywhere.

Restraint

Avoid chart overload, excessive colors, 3D visuals and decorative clutter.

Dashboard QA

  • Every KPI reconciles to SQL.
  • Filters behave correctly.
  • Date slicer affects intended visuals.
  • No broken relationships.
  • Tooltips are useful.
  • Titles explain what the visual answers.
  • Numbers are readable at normal zoom.
  • Dashboard tells a story without verbal explanation.
Rule: Every visual should answer a question. If you cannot state the question, remove the visual.

11 • Insights

Turn charts into business findings

The dashboard is not the final deliverable. The strongest part of the project is the reasoning you demonstrate after seeing the data.

Weak vs. strong insight

WeakStrong
“Electronics has the most sales.”“Electronics contributes X% of revenue, but its average order value is below the portfolio average, suggesting an opportunity to increase basket size through bundles or cross-selling.”
“Some regions sell more.”“Region A generates high order volume but lower value per order, indicating a potential upsell/cross-sell opportunity.”
“Reviews are lower when delivery is late.”“Orders delivered materially later than expected have a lower average review score, suggesting delivery reliability is an important customer-experience lever.”

Insight formula

Observation → Evidence → Business meaning → Recommendation

Never make the recommendation without showing the evidence that led to it.

Find at least 5 strong insights

  • One revenue/trend insight.
  • One product/category insight.
  • One customer insight.
  • One geographic or seller insight.
  • One operational/customer-satisfaction insight.
  • At least one “surprising” finding.

Quantify everything

Instead of “a lot,” use percentages, amounts, rankings, growth rates, averages or differences.

Finding:
Top 10 products account for 31.4% of revenue.

Meaning:
Revenue is meaningfully concentrated among a small product group.

Recommendation:
Monitor availability and pricing of these products closely and
develop complementary-product offers to protect revenue and
increase basket size.
Do not invent numbers. Fill in the actual values only after your analysis is complete.

Example SQL QA

-- Duplicate order IDs
SELECT order_id, COUNT(*)
FROM orders
GROUP BY order_id
HAVING COUNT(*) > 1;

-- Orphan order items
SELECT COUNT(*)
FROM order_items oi
LEFT JOIN orders o
  ON oi.order_id = o.order_id
WHERE o.order_id IS NULL;

Final QA checklist

  • Measures behave correctly under filters.
